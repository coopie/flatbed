# Chapter 1: A visitor in the fog
_Miss Clara Dunmore asks Holmes to save her intended, the clerk Peter Hale, who is held for the murder of the moneylender Silas Garrow._

It was in the November of 1895, in a week when the fog lay so thick upon Baker Street that the lamps burned at noon, that Miss Clara Dunmore came to our rooms. I set down this account at my friend's particular request. He has complained more than once that my stories keep from the reader the very facts on which his conclusions rest, and in this one I have promised him that the reader shall see everything that we saw, and in the order in which we saw it.

She was a slight young woman of two or three and twenty, plainly dressed, with the inky forefinger of one who copies music for her living. She had walked from Southwark, and the fog had left beads of water upon her hat.

"Mr. Holmes," said she, before she had well sat down, "they have taken Peter, and they say that he has killed a man."

Holmes drew his chair nearer the fire and bade her begin at the beginning.

Her story, stripped of the tears with which she told it, was this. Her intended, Mr. Peter Hale, was clerk to Mr. Silas Garrow, a moneylender who kept an office above a chandler's shop in Cutter's Lane, Rotherhithe. On Tuesday morning Hale had come in at seven, as was his habit, and found his master dead upon the floor, struck down with a heavy brass ruler from his own desk. The strongbox stood open. Hale ran for a constable, and by noon Inspector Lestrade had him in a cell.

"And why Mr. Hale?" asked Holmes.

"Because he has a key to the street door, and because his handkerchief was found beside the body. Because he owed Mr. Garrow thirty pounds, which Mr. Garrow advanced him in the spring when my mother was ill. And because a lamplighter saw a light in the office at twenty past ten on Monday night, when Peter was walking home alone."

"Where had he been?"

"With us, at my mother's house in Union Street, until a quarter to ten. He walked home to his lodging in Bermondsey, and his landlady heard him come in at half past. The inspector says that was time enough to go round by Cutter's Lane." She raised her chin. "It is not true, Mr. Holmes. Peter would not strike a dog."

"Had Mr. Garrow other enemies?"

"Every man who owed him money," said she simply. "But there was one in particular. A Captain Lisle came to the office a week ago, and there were high words. Peter heard the captain say that a man like Garrow would be better dead. Peter told the inspector, and the inspector says the captain was at his club the whole of Monday evening."

Holmes had listened with his eyes closed and his fingertips pressed together. Now he opened them.

"Miss Dunmore, the inspector has a clerk with a key, a debt and a handkerchief, which is a great deal more than he usually has, and he will not be easily parted from it. I shall come to Rotherhithe this afternoon. Watson, you have no patient who cannot spare you?"

I had none, and said so; and within the hour we were rattling eastward in a growler, through a fog so dense that the cabman got down and led his horse for the last half mile.
