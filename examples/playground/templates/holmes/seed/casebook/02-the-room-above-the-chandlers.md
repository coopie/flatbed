# Chapter 2: The room above the chandler's
_Holmes examines the office in Cutter's Lane, meets the chandler Jonas Pell, hears the lamplighter, and keeps his suspicion to himself._

Lestrade met us in Cutter's Lane. The street door of the house opened directly upon a narrow stair, and a bell on a coiled spring was fixed above it, which jangled loudly as we came in and again as the door swung shut behind us.

"Mr. Holmes," said the inspector, with no great warmth. "You will find nothing here that I have not. The clerk did it, and I have him."

The office at the head of the stair was a low room with a single window upon the lane. A desk, a high stool for the clerk, a strongbox of the old pattern with its door standing open, and a gas bracket upon the wall made up the whole of its furniture. The body had been taken away, but a dark stain upon the boards showed where Mr. Garrow had lain, between the desk and a narrow door in the back wall.

"Where does that lead?" asked Holmes.

"Down to the chandler's back shop," said Lestrade. "It is locked on the far side, and Mr. Pell, who keeps the shop, tells me it has not been opened in years. We may leave it out of our reckoning."

Holmes made no answer, but went down upon his knees beside the stain, and for some minutes he was busy with his lens. Near the foot of the back door he found a few pale drops upon the boards, which he scraped up with his penknife and folded into an envelope. As he did so I caught the smell of them, which was the smell of mutton fat.

"Candle-wax," said Lestrade. "The clerk lit his way with a candle."

"No doubt," said Holmes. "Was the gas burning when the body was found?"

"It was not. The clerk says the room was dark and cold."

Upon the desk lay a handkerchief marked "P. H.", which Lestrade showed us with some satisfaction; it had been found beside the body. Holmes glanced at it and turned to the strongbox. The key was in its lock, upon Mr. Garrow's own ring. In the box lay a cash-bag holding some forty pounds in notes and gold, and a ledger bound in green cloth, in which the dead man had entered his debtors by name, a leaf to each letter. Five leaves had been torn out, and their ragged stubs remained.

"Forty pounds left lying, and a ledger torn," said Holmes. "Our thief was no thief, Lestrade. He came for a page."

"For his own page," said the inspector. "It comes to the same thing."

We went down and found Mr. Jonas Pell in his shop, among his candles and soap and coils of tarred rope. He was a heavy, pale man with a soft voice, and he answered Holmes civilly enough. He owned the house, and Mr. Garrow had been his tenant these twenty years. He had shut up shop at eight on Monday, gone to bed in his room behind it, and heard nothing. And the door at the top of his back stair? He shook his head. "That has not been opened since my father's time, sir. Rusted fast."

"Does anyone else sleep in the house?"

"Only old Mrs. Abney, in the attic over the office."

"Deaf as a post," said Lestrade, "and no friend to the police. I had nothing out of her."

It was by now past four, and a lamplighter was coming down the lane with his pole. Holmes hailed him, a lean, elderly man named Albert Coyle, and asked what he had seen on Monday night.

"The gas company had been at the main, sir, and the lamps were out all down the lane. I came along relighting them at twenty past ten, and there was a light in Mr. Garrow's window."

"The gas?"

The old man considered. "No, sir, now you ask it. It moved along the window, and back, and it bobbed, the way a candle does when a man carries it. Then it went out."

Lestrade looked at his watch. "The clerk, with his candle," said he. "I have told you so, Mr. Holmes."

We left him at the door. As we walked towards the river, where the fog hung yellow over the water, I asked Holmes what he made of it all.

"I have a suspicion, Watson," said he, "and I shall not name it to you yet. You would write it down, and if I proved wrong you would never let me forget it. There is a good deal about that house that nobody has troubled to ask."
